The Framework

The TIIP Model

An interactive exploration of Traditional Islamically Integrated Psychotherapy — its epistemology, its ontological model of the human psyche, and its four-stage process of change. The journey from distress to qalb salīm: the sound, healthy heart.

Overview

An ancient framework, applied clinically today

Early Muslim scholars treated care of the psyche — ṭibb al-rūḥānī, “medicine of the soul” — as a subdiscipline of medicine, founding one of the world’s earliest documented hospitals in 9th-century Baghdad. TIIP, developed by Hooman Keshavarzi and Fahad Khan and practiced at the Khalil Center, revives this tradition and formalizes it into a clinical model for the modern therapy room.

🕌

Tradition-rooted

Anchored in classical Islamic scholarship on the soul (nafs), the heart (qalb), and human refinement — not religious advice bolted onto Western therapy.

🔬

Evidence-informed

Integrates contemporary clinical science — cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and experiential methods — graded by the strength of their evidence.

❤️‍🩹

Wellness, not just absence of illness

A person with no diagnosis can still be spiritually unwell — afflicted by ḥasad (corrosive envy) or shuḥḥ (avarice). TIIP treats symptom and spiritual affliction together.

Epistemology

Three sources of knowledge, one science of the soul

Unlike frameworks that draw a hard line between “sacred” and “secular,” TIIP treats empirical, rational, and revealed knowledge as equally legitimate — graded by the strength of their evidence rather than their origin.

🔭
al-ʿilm al-ḥissī

Empirical

Knowledge acquired through observation, sensory experience, and clinical study.

🧩
al-ʿilm al-ʿaqlī

Rational

Truths reached through human reason — whether self-evident (badīhī) or inferred (istidlālī).

📖
al-naql

Verified truth from scripture (Qur’ān) and prophetic tradition (Sunnah).

Each source is graded as definitive (qaṭʿī) or probabilistic (ẓannī) — letting TIIP place any piece of knowledge, Western or Eastern, ancient or new, appropriately within a single coherent model of the psyche.

Qalb (قلب) — The Heart

The homeostatic center of the entire model — a container of both health and pathology. Its condition rises and falls with the ʾaql, nafs, iḥsās, and rūḥ that feed into it. The ultimate aim of treatment is qalb salīm: a sound, balanced heart, reached through ittiḥād (integrative unity).

Process of change

Four stages, two parallel journeys

At every stage, the clinician’s mechanism of change is designed to draw out a corresponding principle of change in the patient.

Patient · Compliance (Inqiyād)Clinician · Alliance (Murābaṭah)

The groundwork

The clinician builds a strong therapeutic bond while assessing motivation to change, religiosity, and where in the psyche the dysfunction originates. The patient becomes willing to engage with what therapy will ask of them.

Patient · Self-Awareness (Inkishāf)Clinician · Uncovering (Mukāshafah)

Introspective self-awareness

The clinician walks the patient through the ontological model itself, co-conceptualizing their struggles in its terms. Acting as a psychospiritual mirror, the Rafīq helps the patient truly notice what is happening within.

Patient · Equilibrium (Iʿtidāl)Clinician · Intervention (Muʿālajah)

Psychospiritual equilibrium

The most directive stage. Treatment targets whichever element is the primary source of dysfunction first: contemplation for cognition, expression for emotion, confronting avoidance for behavioral inclination, remembrance for spiritual emptiness.

Patient · Integrative Unity (Ittiḥād)Clinician · Continuity (Muwāṣalah)

Integrative unity

Reached only once a bond is formed, awareness is built, and balance is restored. All elements of the psyche now serve the same purpose — fragmentation gives way to a holistic, integrated experience.

رَفِيق
Rafīq · intimate friend

The therapeutic relationship

Not a hierarchy. A companionship.

TIIP describes the practitioner not as a detached expert but as a Rafīq — an “intimate friend.” The word comes from rifq, meaning gentle companionship: a relationship that is collaborative and authoritative, never hierarchical or cold.

Crucially, the Rafīq is understood to be on their own parallel journey of growth — expected to stay aware of their own deficiencies and biases, and to tend to their own wellbeing so it never bleeds into the care they provide.

Case illustration

The model in practice

Harris, a 30-year-old man, sought therapy for marital conflict and anger outbursts rooted in anxiety and depression. His case shows how every layer of the psyche surfaces — and how each was addressed in turn.

Harris’s primary issue began with his iḥsās — sadness from unmet needs for connection, which surfaced as anxiety and explosive anger whenever he felt overwhelmed or out of control.
Behavioral pattern recognition addressed his inclinations (nafs), while cognitive reframing — rooted in his own religious education — recalibrated the appraisals driving his reactivity (ʾaql).
Spiritual practices — prayer and acceptance of God’s decree — nourished the rūḥ. As the elements realigned, his qalb steadied: symptom scores improved, his anger came under control, and he learned to hold boundaries without swinging to emotional extremes.
